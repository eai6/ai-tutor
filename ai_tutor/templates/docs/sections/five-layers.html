Adaptation is not one project. It is five, with different owners, different durations and different failure modes. Treating them as one is the most common way a country pilot stalls: the engineering finishes in a fortnight and then waits three months for content review.

Layer 1 — Language and register

The tutor speaks the language of the course, not of the interface. That distinction matters: a student may browse the platform in one language and take a geography lesson delivered in another, and the lesson's language wins for the duration of the session.

Adapting a language is more than translation. For a second locale the platform specifies the form of address (the informal register, because the students are thirteen and fourteen), which orthographic standard applies where the language has more than one, which technical vocabulary stays in the standard form of the language, that brand names are not translated, and that the tutor must not switch back to the original language mid-explanation. These are register decisions a native-speaking educator must make; they are then encoded once and applied to both generated content and live tutoring.

Layer 2 — Curriculum

This is the bulk of the work and the only layer where effort scales with ambition. A country supplies syllabus documents and the rights to use them; the pipeline produces draft lessons; subject experts review. Two routes exist: generate from your own syllabus, or import a curriculum pack — a portable bundle of courses, lessons, exit tickets and media built from an existing deployment, carrying no student records at all.

Budget the review, not the generation. Generation is hours of compute; review is expert weeks, and it is what makes the content defensible in front of a school inspectorate.

Layer 3 — Local context

A tutor that explains trade using the tested deployment's island fisheries data to a student two thousand kilometres inland is technically correct and pedagogically worse than useless. The platform keeps a curated library of local facts — economic, geographic, trade, climate, population, industry, sustainable development — tagged by subject, grade and locale, and injects only the matching locale's facts into prompts. One country's facts cannot leak into another country's course, by construction.

A new locale starts with an empty library and the tutor simply grounds less. Seeding it is a short, high-leverage task for subject experts: place names, currency, familiar geography and ecology, the industries students' families work in.

Layer 4 — National structures

Grade names differ — S1–S5 in the Seychelles deployment, a different set entirely in the next country — and school registers differ, the words for "teacher" and "class" differ, and so do the bands a ministry reports attainment in. All of this is configuration, editable by a super-administrator: the grade list, the school list, competency thresholds for Below / Approaching / Meeting / Exceeding Expectation, the point at which the system advises a teacher to move the class on, branding and colours, and the three teacher permission gates.

Layer 5 — Infrastructure and data residency

Where the application runs, where the database lives, and — separately and more importantly — where the language model runs. Section 5 treats this on its own because it is the decision with the least room for later revision.

SEQUENCING

Layers 1, 4 and 5 are prerequisites for Layer 2 and should be finished first. Generating a subject's content before the locale register is settled means regenerating it. Generating it before the model placement decision means generating it against a quality bar you may not keep.
