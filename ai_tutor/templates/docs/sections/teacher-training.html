THE PERMISSION GATES ARE A TRAINING DIAL

The three teacher permissions — edit and publish content, upload curriculum, regenerate a course — are independent and off by default, and they map onto the third tier above. Open a gate for a teacher when they have been trained through it, not on day one. Regeneration rebuilds every lesson and exit ticket in a course, so it is the last gate to open and the one most schools never need.

TRAIN ON YOUR OWN CONTENT, NOT ON THE SAMPLE CURRICULUM

Orientation held before the subject's content has been reviewed trains teachers on the bundled sample curriculum, which is a different product from the one they will teach. Orientation is the last item in Phase 3, after content approval and before the first cohort — not the first item in Phase 1.

Who trains whom

  • Two or three champion teachers per school, identified before orientation and trained in more depth than their colleagues. Two, not one: a single champion who transfers school takes the school's capability with them.
  • A national trainer cohort trained first and most deeply, so that the second wave of schools can be trained in-country without the platform team. This is the difference between a pilot and a programme that can scale.
  • Champion time has to be timetabled. Support that depends on goodwill in a free period stops the week the timetable tightens.
  • Head teachers are trained on the reports, not on the tutoring. What they need is the class readiness view and what its numbers mean for their school.

Teacher time is the largest non-cash cost in the programme: roughly half a day per teacher for orientation, plus champion time for accompaniment and monthly clinics. It is worth costing explicitly, because it is the item most often assumed to be free.

Point teachers at the troubleshooting section of the teacher guide during orientation. It is written from symptoms rather than causes — "my lesson is stuck on generating", "the competency map shows zero for everything", "a student says the tutor is confused", "the grader marked a correct answer wrong" — which is the form a teacher can actually search under pressure. It deflects a large share of what would otherwise reach line 2.

What to measure

  • Weekly active teachers, as a share of teachers trained. The single best predictor of whether the pilot survives the term.
  • Lessons assigned per class per week — whether the platform is in the scheme of work or beside it.
  • Flagged sessions reviewed, and how long they waited. This is a safeguarding measure before it is an engagement one.
  • Content edits per teacher. A teacher who has never edited a lesson either has perfect content or no confidence in the editor, and those need opposite responses.
  • Support requests by category. A rising share of "how do I" is a training gap; a rising share of "this content is wrong" is a content-review gap. They are easy to confuse and call for entirely different action.

THE SIGNAL TO WATCH FOR IN A PILOT

Teachers opening the dashboard between two lessons rather than at the end of term. The overview was deliberately rebuilt to lead with a short ranked list of what needs the teacher now, ahead of any term analytics, because that is the question a teacher actually has in a five-minute gap. Usage in that pattern means the platform has entered the working week; usage only in reporting periods means it has not.

Failure modes specific to training

  • Training held once, before content exists, and never repeated.
  • No timetabled time for champions, so support depends on goodwill.
  • Whole-staff training before a champion cohort has taught with it for a term.
  • Every permission gate opened on day one, including the destructive ones.
  • One champion per school, who then transfers.
  • Treating the in-product assistant as a replacement for line 2 rather than a filter in front of it.
