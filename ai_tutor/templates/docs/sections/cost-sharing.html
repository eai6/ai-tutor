Section 6 says what it costs. This section is about who pays for it, and about whether it is still being paid for in year three. Education technology programmes fail on the second question far more often than on the first.

The cost lines behave differently, so share them differently

Cost lineShapeWho can realistically carry it
Platform infrastructureRecurring, small, and flat — it does not grow with enrolmentCentral. It is the wrong line to devolve to schools, because a school cannot make it smaller by behaving differently.
Model usageRecurring, and the only line that grows with teachingCentral, but reported per school. It is the one line school behaviour actually moves.
Content generation and reviewFront-loaded per subject, then near zero until the syllabus changesBehaves like a capital item — the natural fit for project or partner funding.
Devices and connectivityCapital plus recurring, and highly localSchool, district or an existing device programme. The largest number, and the least specific to this platform.
Teacher timeRecurring, non-cashThe school timetable. Non-cash is not the same as free — see section 10.

Only two of those five are recurring central cash. That pair is the number that has to survive into a ministry budget line, and it is a much easier number to defend than the programme total — which is dominated by devices and by teacher time that the system is already spending.

Four funding models

WRITE THE HANDOVER INTO THE AGREEMENT AT THE START

Model B fails in a predictable way: the pilot succeeds, the partner's funding ends, and the recurring cost arrives in a budget cycle that has already closed. The mitigation is cheap and has to be early — name the recurring figure and the date the ministry assumes it in the original agreement, and re-measure that figure at the end of the pilot against real usage rather than the projection. A handover discussed in the final quarter is a handover that will not happen.

ON CHARGING HOUSEHOLDS

Direct student or household contribution is technically straightforward and strategically self-defeating. The case for the platform is that it gives a tutor to students who cannot buy one; a fee reintroduces exactly the selection it was meant to remove, and it does so at the schools least able to absorb it. If a contribution is unavoidable, put it at school or district level, where it can be equalised, rather than at the household.

Making the recurring line small

The levers in section 6 are cost engineering. Read through a sustainability lens, three of them change the shape of the commitment rather than just its size:

  • A self-hosted model converts recurring cash into capital plus electricity. That is a different budget line, often an easier one to defend, and — unlike per-turn billing — it does not grow when the programme succeeds.
  • The offline builds remove the recurring cloud cost entirely, at the price of hardware and a quality trade-off that section 8 explains how to measure.
  • Per-purpose model selection means the tutoring model, which drives nearly all of the spend, can be changed without touching content generation or the quality judge.

The target is the cheapest configuration that passes the quality gate, not the cheapest configuration. A saving that costs a grade is not a saving, and the harnesses in section 8 exist so that this is a measurement rather than an argument.

A cost-per-student figure you can defend

Boards and treasuries ask for cost per student, and the honest answer is a ratio you derive rather than a number anyone can quote at you. The method:

  1. Take observed model spend for a month — the pilot anchor in section 6 is roughly US$600 across four schools.
  2. Divide by active students, not enrolled ones. A student who took no session cost nothing and proves nothing.
  3. Multiply by the months in the school year, and add the fixed infrastructure line divided across the whole cohort — that second term shrinks as the system grows, which is the main argument for one national instance rather than several.

Worked through with an illustrative 400 active students across those four schools, that is about US$1.50 per active student per month, or roughly US$15 across a ten-month year, before infrastructure. Substitute your own active-student count — the arithmetic is given for its shape, not its total, and the student number is an assumption here rather than an observation.

Sustainability is not only financial

KindWhat it means hereWhat makes it hold
FinancialThe recurring line survives a change of budget cycleA named directorate owns it, not a project code with an end date
TechnicalThe country can operate, upgrade and redeploy its own instanceThe self-hosting paths exist so that operation does not have to be bought. A system that can rebuild its own deployment is not exposed to anyone's continuity
ContentThe curriculum is still current after the next syllabus revisionA funded refresh cycle. Regeneration is cheap; the expert review after it is not, and it is the part that gets forgotten
InstitutionalThe programme survives the people who started itNamed roles rather than named people, two champions per school, and documentation that ships with the product

What to write into the funding agreement

  • The recurring figure, and the date and budget line that assumes it.
  • Who owns the deployment, the data and the credentials when the project ends.
  • Rights to the generated content, and to the syllabus material it derives from.
  • A content refresh cycle tied to the syllabus revision calendar, and who funds the review.
  • An exit clause: what happens to the instance and the data if the programme stops. This is cheap to honour here — the platform runs on the country's own infrastructure and its content exports as a portable pack, so leaving costs a migration rather than a rebuild.
